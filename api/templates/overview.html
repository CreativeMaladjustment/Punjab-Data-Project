The publishing networks of British Punjab, read out of the imperial print register

Every book printed in the Punjab between 1867 and 1942 had to be registered with the colonial government under Act XXV of 1867. The quarterly Catalogues of Books registered in the Punjab record the printer, the publisher, the city, the print run, the price and the language of each one. Twenty-six bound volumes of them sit in the India Office Records as page scans.

This site tracks the work of turning those scans into a queryable record of who printed for whom — and shows what the vision models have extracted, page by page, so it can be checked.

What print runs do and don't measure

Print runs measure publisher supply decisions under a legal-deposit regime — not readership, not literacy.

The catalogue is an instrument of surveillance before it is a bibliography. What it counts is what a printer was willing to declare, in a city where declaring was compulsory. Entries carry the annotator's own editorialising, which is kept verbatim rather than corrected.

Three layers are kept separate throughout: page image, verbatim record, normalised layer. Nothing is silently corrected — uncertain readings are flagged per entry, with stated reasoning, and surfaced in the QC review queue.
